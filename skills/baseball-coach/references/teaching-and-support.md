# Teach the next useful action

Load this reference when a coach needs to explain, troubleshoot, or adapt an activity. Start with beginners around ages 5–8; offer the same entry point to an older beginner. Use the [practice reference](practices.md) for supervision and the [safety reference](rules-safety-sources.md) for stopping and escalation. These are original coaching suggestions, not a clinical assessment or a validated training program.

## Observe before correcting

Describe what happened: “The ball passed before the swing started,” or “The child stepped back when the toss came.” Do not turn one attempt into a trait such as lazy, uncoordinated, anxious, or inattentive. A miss alone does not tell you why it happened.

Before changing mechanics, check:

1. **Understanding:** demonstrate, then invite the child to point to the target or show the starting action. “Do you understand?” may not reveal a misunderstanding. Accept a gesture instead of requiring an explanation.
2. **The adult's feed:** was the pitch, roll, or toss predictable and within comfortable reach? Repeat a usable feed before treating misses as a hitting or catching problem.
3. **Comfort and equipment:** ask simply whether anything hurts or feels uncomfortable. Check usable glove fit, manageable bat, tee position, footing, and required protective gear. Do not ask a child to work through discomfort.
4. **Task demand:** is the distance, ball speed, number of choices, or waiting time too much right now? Make one part easier, then observe again.

Pain, fatigue, distress, or danger overrides the teaching sequence. Stop the activity as needed; pain or fatigue stops throwing. Follow the safety reference and involve the guardian or appropriate medical help. Do not diagnose or use a successful retry as clearance.

Fit equipment to the task within applicable rules: a larger soft practice ball may make interception easier; a smaller grip-friendly ball may suit throwing. “Scaled” does not always mean smaller. The purpose is a manageable task, not purchasing special equipment or guaranteeing learning.

## A small teaching loop

Choose one useful cue, demonstrate it, offer a safe attempt, observe, and retry or adjust. **One cue is a planning heuristic**, intended to keep the next action clear; it is not a scientifically proven limit for every child.

For a roll-and-stop activity: “Move behind the ball.” Show a slow side step and controlled stop. Roll gently to the ready child. Notice whether they moved into its path; do not add five comments about feet, knees, hands, head, and throwing. If the roll was poor, fix the roll. If the child remains unsure, repeat the demonstration or roll straight ahead.

Give assistants a modest reason linked to the task: “We are using a slow roll so the child has time to move behind it.” Avoid invented neurological explanations or claims that a cue prevents injury. Agree on the current cue so adults do not issue competing instructions.

Use a short original analogy only if it helps: “Make a little garage for the ball.” Immediately show the literal action: put the glove down in the rolling ball's path. Drop the analogy if it confuses the child. Keep stop signals, bat rules, and retrieval directions literal.

## Feedback that responds to the child

Name a real action: “You checked that your partner was ready.” When correction is useful, give an achievable next action: “Let's move the target closer.” Avoid praise that ranks children or implies permanent talent.

Allow a comfortable child some attempts without commentary; offer more demonstration or immediate guidance when they are unsure. There is no universal feedback percentage. Where appropriate, ask, “Want a tip or another try?” This choice never delays a safety instruction. Keep explanations short enough to return to play.

A single good attempt shows immediate performance, not lasting learning. Revisit the task on a later day without another explanation to observe retention; an ordinary repeat is not a calibrated test. Use it to plan, not score or certify readiness. Give enough similar attempts to understand the activity, then offer one manageable change. Randomly mixing skills is not a universally better learning recipe.

## Offer support without labels

Provide options to everyone: watch one demonstration, start closer, receive a roll instead of a toss, point instead of answer aloud, or take a supervised pause. Preserve meaningful participation in the shared playing goal; observation or a helper role is an option, not a routine substitute for the child's turns. Adjust one demand at a time. Respect willingness; never force exposure to a feared task. Families and qualified professionals retain responsibility for individual medical plans.

For noise or a busy setting, reduce competing instructions and show the action from a visible position. Use clear markers and consistent words. Offer instruction formats and observe what helps this task today; do not label a child a “visual learner” or “kinesthetic learner,” or rate ability from a few attempts. Ask caregivers privately what practical adjustment helps when needed; do not solicit diagnoses, build private child profiles, or put health details in shared plans. Record only an anonymous task note if useful: “Child asked for a closer roll; offer that option.”

## Four ordinary troubleshooting moments

These are possibilities to check, not explanations inferred from appearance. After an offered retry, watch both the action and comfort; keep, simplify, pause, or stop.

| Notice | Check, then try one adjustment |
|---|---|
| Child does something different after a long explanation | Ask them to show the starting action. Words may be unclear; show one action and say “Start here.” |
| Child leaves the waiting marker | Check whether the queue is long or the boundary unclear. Shorten turns while preserving supervision; demonstrate the waiting place. |
| Child turns away from a toss | Ask whether they want another try and check feed speed, glare, and discomfort. Offer a slow roll: “Watch it come.” Do not infer fear from a face. |
| Child is upset after a miss | Ask whether they want a pause or easier attempt. Quietly offer “Let's try it closer.” Keep support observable and interruptible, never isolated. |

## What supports this approach

- **Professional developmental guidance:** [AAP sports readiness guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sports/Pages/Is-Your-Child-Ready-for-Sports.aspx) supports basic activities, demonstration, play, and adjusting demands to readiness. It does not assess an individual child.
- **Research synthesis:** [van der Veer and colleagues, 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0264873) reviewed 13 heterogeneous, low-quality studies. Child-controlled feedback timing showed promise for retention; reduced feedback frequency was inconsistent, and continuous feedback sometimes helped movement quality. This is not proof that external-focus cues or less feedback always work best, or a baseball-specific prescription.
- **Other research syntheses:** [Buszard and colleagues, 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4887542/) supports considering equipment/task scaling, but its 25 studies were mainly basketball/tennis and only five examined learning. [Graser and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6342307/) found mostly conflicting or absent evidence across 25 papers on children's practice order. Neither supplies a universal baseball progression.
- **Original design choices:** the teaching loop, sample wording, adjustment checklist, and anonymous note above are practical applications, not tested guarantees. Sources checked 2026-10-11; recheck consequential safety guidance when used.
