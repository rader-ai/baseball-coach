# Practices that work with the adults you have

Choose one or two goals: for example, stop a rolling ball and run through first base. Show each activity briefly, then let children try. Praise observable actions: “You waited until your partner was looking.” Avoid public ability rankings, elimination games, and exercise as punishment.

Use a simple progression: show one fundamental, let children try an easy version, then change one demand when control and comfort allow. Keep the same cue rather than adding a list of corrections. After a miss, calmly reset: “Let's try it closer.” Offer a small choice, such as a straight or side roll, without making children earn belonging or playtime. For an overwhelmed child, reduce the task and allow a pause; do not label the behavior or infer a diagnosis.

For a complete session, also read [practice operations](practice-operations.md): separate adult setup/prebrief from player time, brief approved helpers with an [assistant card](../assets/assistant-card.md), and budget interruptions. One cue, a demonstration, observation, and an adjusted retry are practical design choices, not proof that less feedback or one cue style always works best.

For correction or individual support, read [teaching and support](teaching-and-support.md). For a specific skill's progression, read [technical progressions](technical-progressions.md); do not load either for a simple scheduling request.

## Build the field before building the schedule

Mark an equipment spot, water/waiting area, and activity boundaries. Inspect footing and nearby traffic. Use suitable soft practice balls when appropriate; softness does not remove the need for separation or protective equipment. Confirm league equipment requirements. Available equipment need not all be used: omit batting when required protective gear or suitable supervision is missing.

For batting, establish one swing area and a waiting area outside the bat's reach and likely ball paths. Only the called batter holds a bat; other bats stay with the adult. No child catches behind a hitter in these beginner drills. The adult controls tee placement and retrieval: bat parked, everyone stopped, then collect. Do not send children into a live hitting area.

For throws, use parallel lanes with all throws going toward a clear target end. No crossing paths. Receivers show readiness before a throw. Retrieve only on a stop signal with all balls held. Use adult rolls or short underhand tosses if partner catching is not ready.

Two adults can supervise two groups only when each has a clear view of their own group and the activities cannot interfere. Keep groups small enough for those adults to manage; player count alone does not prove supervision is adequate. Without safe separation, use one whole-group activity with one adult instructing and the other supporting.

With one adult, keep everyone in a visible group. Only one ball or bat activity is live at a time; waiting children have a simple observation task, not an independent throwing drill. Safeguarding permission and a workable interruption plan are prerequisites. For example, if the league permits it and guardians agree to remain available on site, stop play and hand a child to their own guardian for restroom care while retaining the group. Confirm this arrangement before accepting children; permission for solo group supervision alone does not establish restroom coverage. If either permission or coverage is absent, obtain approved coverage or postpone the on-field session.

## Small activity library

| Activity | Setup and action | One cue | Easier / harder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roll and stop | Adult rolls to one ready child; child stops ball and rolls it back. Others wait outside the lane. | “Get your body behind it.” | Slower, closer roll / gentle roll to either side |
| Step and throw | Child throws one soft ball toward a broad unoccupied target; adult controls collection. | “Step toward the target.” | Underhand toss closer / slightly smaller target, then more distance if controlled |
| Ready catch | Adult gives a gentle short toss to one ready child; no surprise throws. | “Show ready hands.” | Roll or one bounce / gentle toss with a small step |
| Tee contact | Adult sets tee for comfortable contact; one batter swings into an empty sector. | “Look at the ball.” | Larger soft ball or supported setup / choose a broad target direction |
| First-base run | One child runs a clear lane through a flat first-base marker, then follows a separate walk-back path. | “Run through the base.” | Walk route first / react to a coach's “go” |
| Field and choose | Adult rolls one ball; child names the target before a controlled throw. | “Look, then throw.” | Name the target beforehand / add a second target after the first choice is understood |

Keep each adjustment independent: change distance OR pace OR decision complexity. Make a nonthrowing or observing role available without singling a child out. Ask caregivers privately about access needs; don't diagnose them.

## Scheduling and adjustment

Deliver a compact field card before optional preparation details. Separate pre-arrival adult setup from the player clock. Include children's demonstrations, retrieval, encouragement, and all group swaps in player time. Choose duration from readiness, goals, staffing, conditions, and breaks; the sample lengths are not official or optimal age bands. The 32-minute session is an intentionally abbreviated option. Water is freely available; scheduled breaks are reminders, not restrictions.

Use the [opportunity check](practice-operations.md): compare estimated attempts and waits with what the user wants children to practice. A schedule that adds up can still underserve its goal. If opportunities are thin, inspect feasible adult-led lanes, simplify the route, rebalance blocks, or narrow the goal. Reducing repetitions alone does not fix too much waiting. Keep actual ball attempts distinct from supervised shadow movements; neither faster feeds nor unsupervised stations are solutions.

For weather, reserve an option to cut activity and extend shade/rest, subject to current policy and conditions. A shortened plan is not permission to practice when cancellation is required. If an adult leaves, stop first, recount children, secure equipment, and switch only to a permitted, feasible whole-group plan.

## Always finish active practice with a fun game

Reserve a short final game in every plan. Give it a name, simple playable rules, adult roles, and an easier option. Reuse today's basic skill rather than teaching a complicated new contest. Everyone stays involved; no elimination, penalty laps, forced competition, or waiting alone. Keep the same bat/ball controls as in drills. A cheer or discussion does not replace this active game.

Check that a serial game's slower turn cycle leaves time for every child plus instruction and margin. When it doesn't, prefer a simultaneous ball-free game in safe personal spaces; don't knowingly schedule only enough turns for the quickest children.

For example, play **Baseball Statues**: with bats and balls secured, children stay in their own spaces while the adult calls “ready,” “freeze,” or “point to first.” Everyone makes the pose; let children suggest a silly safe statue. Demonstrate each call for beginners; seated pointing works too. One adult can lead while keeping the whole group in view.

When a session needs a routine time cut, shorten earlier blocks and preserve a brief game and wrap-up. Stop or cancel immediately when safety requires; never continue to satisfy the fun finish. After the game, ask one optional question (“What helped you get ready?”), appreciate a real action, and complete supervised handoff. Optional home play should be short, low-pressure, caregiver-supervised, and possible without buying equipment.

Development note: [AAP guidance on sports readiness](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sports/Pages/Is-Your-Child-Ready-for-Sports.aspx) supports adapting to developmental readiness, brief demonstrations and play for younger children, and learning over winning. [Little League's coach-pitch guidance](https://www.littleleague.org/play-little-league/coach-pitch/) emphasizes fundamentals and a positive team experience. These inform the approach; the specific games and final-game requirement are design choices, not a validated treatment or guaranteed performance method. Both checked 2026-10-11.
