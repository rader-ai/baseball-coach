# Technical progressions: choose the next useful task

Choose **one or two goals per practice**, using [practice operations](practice-operations.md), [teaching and support](teaching-and-support.md), and [safety guidance](rules-safety-sources.md). Load only relevant sections. A ten-year-old beginner may use a six-year-old's entry task. Progress follows understanding, comfort, willingness, and controlled action today; no fixed success percentage certifies readiness.

## Controls shared by every card

Assign an approved adult to each active group. Keep waiting places outside swing, throwing, landing, and running paths. One adult runs one activity with everyone visible, subject to league permission and an interruption plan. A feeder cannot supervise another station. Inspect equipment and footing; use required protective gear. Soft balls still require separation.

Only the called batter holds a bat. Keep **every part of the feeder outside full bat reach**, including feeding hands. Hard-ball front feeds need an appropriate, properly positioned protective screen; otherwise use a tee or safely arranged soft-ball activity. This conservative planning control is not a universal league rule. No child catches behind these hitters. Park bats and hold balls before retrieval or lane crossings.

Stop for pain, fatigue, distress, unsafe feeds, or anyone entering the active lane. Never surprise a child with a throw, ask them to spin to find a pop-up, or race two children for one ball. Establish ownership before introducing a second defender. Increase only one demand at a time, and revisit comparable attempts later; immediate improvement does not prove retention. Regressions below are task changes, never pain treatment.

## 1. Warm-up and ready movement

**Goal/setup:** Prepare to move and respond in a clear space. Bats and balls stay secured. An adult demonstrates while children have separate visible movement spots; walk the boundaries first.

**Look for:** Can the child stop on the signal? Can they move toward a marker without entering a neighbor's space?

**Check first:** Unclear words, uneven ground, cramped spacing, or fatigue may explain difficulty. Change the space or demonstration before correcting posture.

**Cue/why:** “Move, then freeze.” Practicing a controlled stop helps organize the activity. Show comfortable walking, gentle jogging, and side steps; do not impose a deep crouch or exhausting warm-up.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat the route. Easier: walk or use accessible movement/pointing. Harder: choose between two named markers without racing. Play **Ready Shapes**, following safe movement calls. Stop if balance, comfort, or space makes movement unsafe.

## 2. Overhand throw

**Goal/setup:** Send a comfortable throw toward a broad, unoccupied target in a marked lane. The adult controls turns and collection; begin without a child receiver.

**Look for:** Does the child identify the target before throwing? Does the step and throw travel generally toward it with a controlled finish?

**Check first:** Try a closer/larger target or a ball the child can grip comfortably within applicable rules. Check where the target is and whether the instruction was understood before changing the arm.

**Cue/why:** “Step toward your target.” Demonstrate an easy opposite-foot step to help direct the action. Do not force an elbow height, wrist snap, or identical arm slot.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat, noticing direction and comfort. Easier: underhand toss. Harder: more distance OR a narrower target. Play **Choose a Window**, selecting a target before throwing. Avoid maximum-effort contests, weighted balls, and velocity goals.

## 3. Receiving a throw

**Goal/setup:** Receive a predictable soft toss from an adult in an isolated lane. The child signals readiness; the adult tosses gently within reach and waits before the next feed.

**Look for:** Is the child ready before release? Do hands or glove move toward the ball's reachable path?

**Check first:** Reduce feed speed or distance; check glove fit, glare, and willingness. A poor feed is not a catching fault.

**Cue/why:** “Show your ready glove.” Demonstrate an open target, then comfortable movement to receive. Two hands can help secure a routine reachable catch; do not demand two hands on every reach or put the bare hand in front of the incoming ball.

**Retry/progress:** Offer the same usable toss. Easier: rolling ball or gentle bounce. Harder: one small step to a known side; partner catch only when both children can send and receive manageable throws under supervision. Play **Ready Signal**: the receiver chooses when to invite the feed. Pause if they turn away or decline; never force repeated exposure.

## 4. Grounder to controlled throw

**Goal/setup:** Stop an adult's slow roll, secure it, then send it toward one clear target. Begin without a runner; waiting children remain outside both paths.

**Look for:** Does the child move into the routine roll's path? Is the ball controlled before the throw starts?

**Check first:** Smooth the rolling surface or slow/straighten the feed. Use a larger soft ball if useful; separate stopping from throwing when the combined task is confusing.

**Cue/why:** “Stop it, then find your target.” The pause gives the child time to control the ball and choose a direction. Demonstrate comfortable knees/hips and glove near the ground, without forcing a rigid pose. Getting behind a routine roll is useful; do not demand that a child block a hard bad hop with their body.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat the same roll. Easier: stop and roll back. Harder: a gentle side roll OR a target choice. Play **Delivery Stop** with one named destination. A knee-grounder activity is optional only if comfortable, and should return to whole-body movement; never use kneeling to correct pain.

## 5. Fly-ball tracking

**Goal/setup:** Follow a gentle loft in clear space with one active child and one adult. Start with a soft ball, predictable landing area, and no bats or competing fielders.

**Look for:** Does the child follow the visible flight? Can they move toward the landing area without losing balance or entering another lane?

**Check first:** Reduce height, travel, or glare; offer tracking without catching first. Ask whether another attempt is welcome. Turning away does not establish a diagnosis or prove fear.

**Cue/why:** “Find where it lands.” Show the flight and safe movement before asking for a catch. This keeps the first task about tracking rather than a rushed collision with the ball.

**Retry/progress:** Let a gentle toss land in the empty area and have the child point to it. Easier: follow a short bounce or roll. Harder: catch a predictable soft loft, then introduce a small known direction change. Play **Landing Spot** without points or races. Stop for poor visibility or backward stumbling; reset the starting position instead of adding height.

## 6. Tee contact

**Goal/setup:** Send a ball off a tee into an empty sector. The adult checks helmet, bat, tee, footing, waiting zone, and full swing clearance before handing over the bat.

**Look for:** Can the child start and finish a swing within their space? Does the bat reach the ball's position?

**Check first:** Adjust tee height/location for comfortable contact; check whether the bat is manageable and the ball visible. Demonstrate without a child nearby before correcting the swing.

**Cue/why:** “Send the ball into that space.” A broad target gives the swing a simple purpose. Avoid forced back-elbow poses, “squish the bug,” prescribed power angles, or one bat path for every ball height.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat with the same setup and observe contact and balance; head movement alone does not establish where eyes are looking. Easier: larger soft practice ball or a comfortable tee adjustment. Harder: choose between broad target sectors without demanding precise placement. Play **Pick Your Field**. Park the bat before collecting; stop when control or clearance is lost.

## 7. Moving-ball contact

**Goal/setup:** Connect with a predictable adult feed after the child understands the batting routine. Apply the full feeder-clearance and screen controls above. One batter, no child catcher, no simultaneous retrieval.

**Look for:** Does the child become ready before the feed? Does the swing begin early or late relative to a usable ball's arrival?

**Check first:** Examine feed consistency, speed, height, visibility, and ball/bat suitability. An unreachable pitch says little about mechanics. Agree on a ready signal and use a manageable feed before adding advice.

**Cue/why:** “Get ready, then meet the ball.” Demonstrate the sequence so timing has a clear starting point. Do not prescribe an arm, hip, or eye correction from one miss.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat a comparable feed. Easier: return to the tee or slow the feed in a safe arrangement. Harder: modestly vary location OR pace only after comfort with the predictable task. Play **Ready to Hit**, letting the batter invite each feed. No hit quota requires continued swings when tired or distressed.

## 8. Baserunning

**Goal/setup:** Learn the route and run through first under adult control. Use flat markers and a separate return path; initially no balls, bats, defenders, or sliding.

**Look for:** Does the child travel toward the correct base? Do they continue through first before slowing in the clear space beyond?

**Check first:** Walk the route; check marker visibility, footwear, congestion, and the meaning of “through.” Route confusion is not laziness.

**Cue/why:** “Run past first, then slow down.” Demonstrate a controlled finish so the child does not stop suddenly at the marker.

**Retry/progress:** Walk, then repeat at a comfortable run. Easier: shorter approach or accessible route. Harder: follow a separate coach signal for stopping at another base, taught first at walking pace. Play **Base Postcards**: name the destination, then travel there. Do not teach that a left turn after first automatically means an out; rules depend on what the runner does and the applicable division. Collision, sliding, and live-play rules need separate verified instruction.

## 9. Field and choose; force versus tag

**Goal/setup:** Connect a fielded ball to a simple game decision. Begin with an adult roll and one named target; use markers to represent runners rather than a live race.

**Look for:** Can the child identify the intended destination? Do they control the ball and check the receiving area before throwing?

**Check first:** Remove extra choices and explain the situation by walking it. If “force” or “tag” is unfamiliar, pause throwing and demonstrate with stationary markers.

**Cue/why:** “Know where, then throw.” Choosing first simplifies the action. Walk a batter-runner going to first: with possession, the defender can touch first before arrival. Then show a runner off a base without a force: touching the base alone is insufficient; a legal tag needs the ball, or glove holding it, touching the runner. Verify the actual situation's rules.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat the same scenario. Easier: point to the destination without throwing. Harder: choose between two clearly explained situations. Play **Coach's Puzzle** without live runners or contact. Do not turn an explanation into chasing or hard tags; add live play only with understood rules, space, and supervision.

## 10. Defensive positions and backup

**Goal/setup:** Learn that players have starting areas and useful jobs away from the ball. Walk positions with bats secured; initially use no ball, then one adult-controlled roll.

**Look for:** Does the child recognize their starting area? Can they name or demonstrate their assigned job without crowding another fielder?

**Check first:** Too many position names or moving players may be the problem. Reduce to “field this ball” and one separate backup place; use visible markers.

**Cue/why:** “Your job is here.” Point and walk the route so ownership is clear. Backup means a planned position beyond a possible missed play, with a safe route; it is not everyone pursuing the ball.

**Retry/progress:** Rehearse at walking pace. Easier: one defender and pointing observers. Harder: two defined roles with one active ball after ownership and communication are understood. Rotate roles without public ability rankings. Play **Find Your Job** with a freeze and explanation before each roll. Never introduce an extra live ball simply to keep fielders busy.

## 11. Relays and cutoffs

**Goal/setup:** Understand passing a ball through an intermediate teammate. This is an optional upward path, not a beginner requirement. Walk the roles first; use one lane and one ball, with receivers facing the sender before any throw.

**Look for:** Does the intermediate player identify the next target? Do they secure the ball before turning and sending it onward?

**Check first:** Shorten distances or replace throws with handoffs. Check whether each player knows whose turn it is; do not demand faster exchanges to fix confusion.

**Cue/why:** “Catch, turn, find.” A clear sequence separates possession from the next action. The adult defines the destination; advanced cutoff decisions can wait.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat a walking handoff sequence. Easier: child passes to an adult who demonstrates the next action. Harder: short controlled throws through the same sequence, then one destination choice. Play **Message Delivery**, completing a shared route rather than racing. Stop if a receiver is turned away or lanes overlap; never send a second ball into the sequence.

## 12. Pitching boundaries

**Goal/setup:** An eligible, willing child explores a comfortable delivery toward a broad target with an approved adult. Child pitching is optional. Verify rules, workload history, rest, and position restrictions first; unknown history means no clearance to pitch.

**Look for:** Does the child understand when to start and stop? Can they finish an easy delivery balanced and comfortable?

**Check first:** Confirm fatigue, recent activity, distance, footing, and target clarity before mechanics. Never use a delivery change to work around pain.

**Cue/why:** “Throw comfortably toward the target.” Keep the goal understandable without prescribing a universal leg lift or arm slot.

**Retry/progress:** A permitted easy attempt informs instruction, not medical safety. Easier: ball-free demonstration. Harder: qualified youth instruction after verified eligibility, not extra effort or volume. Exclude velocity training, specialty-pitch prescriptions, and professional imitation. Use current safety guidance; rule ceilings are not practice targets.

## 13. Catcher introduction

**Goal/setup:** Introduce receiving as a distinct role with an approved adult, correctly fitted required catcher equipment, and no batter. Begin with a comfortable stance demonstration and gentle predictable feed only when appropriate. General glove-catching practice is not authorization to catch a pitcher.

**Look for:** Can the child present a target comfortably? Can they receive a manageable feed and recover without losing balance?

**Check first:** Inspect gear fit, feed quality, fatigue, and willingness. A painful or unstable stance needs a stop and appropriate help, not deeper squatting.

**Cue/why:** “Show the glove where you're ready.” Give the feeder a visible target. Stances vary with comfort and task; one-knee catching is neither a universal instruction nor universally wrong.

**Retry/progress:** Repeat a comfortable no-batter setup. Easier: ball-free demonstration or ordinary standing receiving. Harder: qualified instruction in movement, blocking, and throws with verified gear and workload rules. Do not fire balls at a beginner to teach toughness, demand body sacrifice, or add a hitter as a reward. Stop for discomfort, fatigue, or inadequate protection.

## Evidence and observation limits

These cards are **original coaching adaptations**, not experimentally validated prescriptions. Little League's [throwing](https://www.littleleague.org/university/articles/tee-ball-drills-team-throwing/), [fielding](https://www.littleleague.org/university/articles/tee-ball-drills-fielding/), [tee hitting](https://www.littleleague.org/university/articles/tee-ball-drills-tee-hitting/), [baserunning](https://www.littleleague.org/university/articles/tee-ball-drills-run-the-bases/), and [defensive rotations](https://www.littleleague.org/university/articles/tee-ball-drills-defensive-rotations/) are coaching resources, not outcome trials. [Catching 101](https://www.littleleague.org/university/articles/catching-101/) supports role/stance distinctions; its adult performance language is not a beginner standard. [Runner FAQs](https://www.littleleague.org/help-center/rule-7-00-the-runner-faqs/) are a starting point for Little League rule questions, not a complete ruling on every first-base play. Verify the actual governing rule and facts. Sources checked 2026-10-11. Older curricula do not override current safety rules; inaccessible videos and account-only manuals are not claimed as reviewed.

No photo or clip is required. Describe visible actions and uncertainty only. A still, blur, cropped feet/ball/release, unknown mirroring, missing target, unusual feed, or one attempt cannot establish a mechanical diagnosis. Do not infer pain, injury risk, eye fixation, or calibrated angles. Seek comparable attempts and the child's comfort report; preserve privacy.
