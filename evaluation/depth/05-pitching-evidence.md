We can give your nine-year-old a useful 30-minute baseball session today, but **his pitching eligibility is unverified, so I cannot clear him for a pitching station** while yesterday's workload is unknown. Make today's goals comfortable movement and learning a base route. We can acknowledge his interest in pitching with a brief ball-free introduction; skip harder-throwing, radar, weighted-ball, long-distance, and maximum-effort work.

Unknown pitching history does not automatically prohibit all ordinary catch. However, it also does not tell us how much additional throwing is sensible today. The complete plan below deliberately uses no throwing while you resolve the history; that is a conservative planning choice, not a claim that a universal rule bans catch. If he reports pain or fatigue, stop throwing and do not use mechanics changes to push through it. Tell the guardian and seek appropriate medical assessment for pain or injury concerns.

**What to verify before a future child-pitching session:**

- The organization, baseball division, season, regular-season versus tournament rules, and local pitching/practice restrictions. Confirm league age using that organization's method; ordinary age nine may not settle it.
- Actual pitching dates and official totals from yesterday and the relevant prior days, across every team, game, and event. Check records with the responsible adults instead of assuming a missing count means zero. Establish required rest and whether he has pitched on consecutive days.
- Catching innings and pitcher/catcher or other position restrictions, plus applicable same-day/multiple-game rules.
- Additional arm workload from warm-ups, practice, lessons, ordinary catch, and catching throws; recent rest, current comfort/fatigue, and any existing clinician restrictions. These activities add load, but do not invent a formula converting them to official game pitch counts.
- Safe space, approved adult coverage, appropriate target/receiver arrangement and protective equipment, and local weather/field permission.

For an example of why records matter, Little League's current regular-season baseball Regulation VI has age-based daily limits, rest thresholds, and catcher/pitcher restrictions. For league age 14 and under, 21–35 pitches generally requires one calendar day of rest; 36–50 requires two. The full provision includes threshold exceptions and other restrictions, so a partial count or this example cannot clear him. Limits are ceilings, not session targets or medical clearance. [Little League regular-season pitching rules, checked October 11, 2026](https://www.littleleague.org/playing-rules/pitch-count/).

[MLB/USA Baseball Pitch Smart for ages 9–12](https://www.mlb.com/pitch-smart/pitching-guidelines/ages-9-12), checked October 11, 2026, supplements league rules with rest and fatigue guidance; it does not grant eligibility.

**Today's 30-minute session.** This assumes one child, an involved guardian and a coaching adult in an observable, interruptible setting that meets the organization's safeguarding rules. For family-only play, the caregiver supervises directly. If this is a team event, required approved coverage must be in place before accepting the child. Use a clear movement area, cones or flat markers, water, and comfortable footwear. No bat, thrown ball, catcher, or live defender is needed. Check local conditions first.

Allow **six adult minutes before the player clock**: three to inspect footing and set a clear approach to a flat “first base” marker with room to slow beyond it and a separate return route; three to agree on supervision, STOP, water, care needs, and pickup. An early arrival stays with their guardian until accepted. Adults remain present; do not leave the child to obtain workload records during the session.

| Minutes | What to do |
|---|---|
| 0–4 | Welcome and simple comfort check. Say, “We need your throwing record before pitching. Today we'll explore the setup and play a running game.” Show the boundaries and STOP signal; ask the child to point to the safe waiting/rest place. |
| 4–7 | Easy walking, gentle side steps, and comfortable movement in clear space. Cue: “Move, then freeze.” Demonstrate for 30 seconds; give chances to stop. Easier: walk or accessible seated movement. No exhausting warm-up. |
| 7–13 | **Pitcher's planning spot, without throwing:** adult briefly demonstrates choosing a target, waiting for readiness, and a comfortable step/balanced finish with no ball. Child can point to a large target marker, walk into a comfortable step, and pause. Keep arms relaxed; no repeated forceful shadow deliveries or exact arm positions. Cue: “Step toward that marker.” First minute demo, four minutes short attempts with pauses/choices, final minute reset. Watch comfortable balance and understanding, not simulated velocity. Easier: point and watch. If this bores him, use the time for the base-route walk below. |
| 13–16 | Water/rest and transition. Ask whether he wants another demonstration or the running activity. |
| 16–23 | **First-base route:** walk from the start to and through the flat marker, slow in the clear area, then use the separate walk-back route. No balls, defenders, tags, or sliding. Cue: “Run past first, then slow down.” One minute demo, five minutes comfortable turns/rest, one minute reset. If he stops abruptly on the marker, first check what “past” means by walking it again. Easier: shorter approach or walk; harder: a comfortable jog after the route is understood, with no racing. |
| 23–27 | **Final fun game: Baseball Statues.** Adult calls “ready,” “point to first,” or “freeze”; the child makes the pose in their own clear space. Alternate choosing a silly safe statue. Thirty seconds demo, three minutes play, thirty seconds finish/reset. No elimination or scoring. Easier: seated poses or pointing. Adult watches spacing and comfort throughout. |
| 27–30 | Optional “Which activity would you like again?”, specific encouragement for an observed action, and guardian recap/handoff. Explain what workload information is still needed. Supervision continues until handoff is complete. |

If attention fades, shorten an earlier block and use the next two minutes for Baseball Statues and two minutes for wrap-up, with a minute to secure/regroup if needed. If pain, fatigue, unsafe conditions, or lost coverage requires stopping, end safely without insisting on the game. A late start comes out of the six-minute planning spot and seven-minute route block; retain the boundary demo, water, and supervised closing. After handoff, take **three adult minutes** to note what he understood and the next adjustment, plus **two minutes** to collect markers.

**How to use cues and feedback without overstating the science.** An external-focus cue directs attention to an effect or destination, such as “send it toward the target”; a body-focused cue names a movement, such as “step with this foot.” Neither category is a guarantee. Today's marker and base cues are simple options to try, not an evidence-proven recipe for every child.

The cited 2022 review by van der Veer and colleagues studied ways of delivering externally focused instruction/feedback. It included 13 heterogeneous studies of low methodological quality. Reduced feedback frequency had inconsistent results; child-controlled timing showed some promise for retention, and limited findings sometimes favored continuous feedback for movement quality. Its comparisons largely varied delivery within external-focus approaches, so it cannot establish external focus as universally superior to body-focused instruction. It also does not establish an optimal baseball feedback percentage. [Published review, checked October 11, 2026](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0264873).

My practical application is to show one action, offer a short cue, watch, and adjust to his response. Give a comfortable child some quiet tries; give an unsure child another demonstration or useful immediate correction. You can ask, “Want a tip or another try?” Safety directions are immediate regardless of that choice. Praise a real action—“You slowed down in the clear space”—instead of ranking talent. A good attempt today is immediate performance, not proof of lasting learning; revisit an ordinary comparable task later. This session promises neither improved velocity nor injury prevention.

Once history, rest, and rules are verified, an eligible, willing, comfortable child can explore an easy delivery toward a broad target with a qualified youth coach and suitable supervision. Start with “Throw comfortably toward the target,” observe comfort/control, and avoid forcing a universal elbow height, arm slot, or leg lift. More effort is not the automatic next step. AAP guidance emphasizes balancing total activity load and recovery; consult the child's clinician for individual medical concerns.
