# Helping an eight-year-old who misses and turns away

**Do not upload a named video. We can begin with an anonymous description, and a video is not needed for this next step.** Missing pitches and turning away are observations; they do not establish laziness, a mechanical fault, fear, or a diagnosis. Pause pitching if she is turning away or uncomfortable, and ask whether she wants to continue.

Tell the assistant, out of the child's hearing but within the team's normal observable setting:

> “Please describe what happened without calling her lazy. We saw misses and turning away. We need to check our pitch and the task before changing her swing. I'll give one cue; please watch and tell me what you actually see. Five fixes at once won't give her one clear next action or help us know which change was useful.”

One cue is a practical choice for this moment, not a scientifically established instruction limit for all children.

## What the assistant should watch

Only observe live attempts if she willingly chooses them and the setup is safe; do not keep pitching just to collect evidence. Begin with what you already saw, acknowledging what you cannot tell.

| Observe neutrally | What to check before correcting her |
|---|---|
| “The feed arrived before she showed she was ready.” | Did the adults agree on a ready signal and wait for it? Ask her to show the starting action rather than simply asking whether she understands. |
| “That ball passed above/outside comfortable reach.” | Feed height, direction, speed, and consistency. An unusable pitch tells us little about her swing. |
| “On the reachable pitch, the swing began after the ball had passed,” if actually visible. | Timing may be difficult at this speed. Do not convert one late swing into a fixed mechanical diagnosis. |
| “She stepped back/turned away as the feed approached.” | Ask about comfort and willingness. Check glare, visibility, footing, protective-gear fit, and whether the bat is manageable. A head turn does not prove where her eyes were fixed or why she moved. |

Choose just one or two of these observations for a short sequence. Do not narrate all of them to the child. Unknown feed quality, an unclear start, a fast-moving target, unsuitable equipment, tiredness, discomfort, or an unpleasant experience are possibilities to check—not conclusions to assign. Her own account matters; she does not have to explain a feeling to earn a pause.

## One safe adjustment: replace the moving feed with a tee

Offer: **“Want to try a ball that stays still, or take a break?”** If she chooses the tee, use a comfortable tee position, a suitable soft ball, a manageable bat, and the required correctly fitted helmet. If a tee/safe setup is unavailable, pause this hitting task rather than holding the ball in someone's hand or forcing more pitches.

One approved adult tends the batter; another approved adult supervises any waiting children. Keep everyone outside full bat reach and likely ball paths, with only the called batter holding the bat. The adult steps clear before the swing; park the bat before approaching the tee or collecting. No child catcher and no simultaneous retrieval. A quiet conversation remains observable and interruptible; do not isolate the child.

For Little League specifically, batting helmets are required at practice and on-deck bat handling is prohibited in Tee Ball, Minor, and Major divisions. Confirm the full requirements for your actual league/division; its identity was not supplied. [Little League Safety Code](https://www.littleleague.org/playing-rules/appendices/appendix-b/), accessed October 11, 2026.

Demonstrate one comfortable swing safely, with her watching outside the zone. Give **one cue: “Send the ball into that space.”** Point to a broad empty sector. A stationary ball removes the moving-feed timing demand, and the broad target gives the swing a purpose. Neither choice guarantees contact or explains her earlier turning away. Do not layer on elbow, hip, foot, head, and eye instructions.

The assistant can use this short card:

> **WATCH:** Can she start and finish within her space? Does the bat reach the stationary ball? Ask her about comfort instead of inferring it from appearance.
>
> **SAY:** Use the agreed cue only if needed: “Send the ball into that space.” Let some willing attempts happen without commentary. Praise a real action: “You waited until the area was clear.”
>
> **CHECK:** After a few comparable, willing attempts, ask “Does this feel okay? Another try or a break?” Keep tee position/ball/target the same long enough to see what happens. There is no required number of swings or hits.
>
> **STOP:** Pain, fatigue, distress, unsafe bat control, an occupied sector, or lost supervision overrides the teaching plan. Follow league procedures and involve the guardian/appropriate medical help as needed. A better swing is not medical clearance.

## How to decide whether to keep it

- **Keep the tee task for now** if she wants another turn, reports it feels comfortable, stays within the swing space, and the task looks more manageable—for example, the bat reaches the ball more often or she can prepare without backing away. Contact alone is not the only useful observation.
- **Pause or simplify further** if she turns away again, declines, remains distressed, or cannot safely manage the setup. Ask what would make the activity feel more workable; a supervised break or watching a demonstration is a real choice, not a penalty or permanent substitute for her turns. Do not answer an unsuccessful adjustment by adding five fixes.
- **Revisit on another day** with the same manageable setup and minimal prompting. A good attempt today shows immediate performance; repeating the action later offers better information about what she has retained. Neither is a readiness certificate. When she is comfortable and wants to try a moving ball again, introduce one predictable safe feed and a clear ready signal under the proper safety setup, rather than increasing several demands together.

An optional private, anonymous coaching note could be: “Stationary ball offered; child chose three tries, reported comfortable; bat reached ball twice. Offer same choice next session.” Write only what happened; omit identifiers and speculative diagnoses. Do not compare her hit rate publicly with teammates.

## Words for the child

> “I noticed you turned away when the ball came. How did that feel? Does anything hurt or feel uncomfortable? You can point or tell me. You don't have to keep swinging. We can try a ball that stays still, watch me once, or take a break.”
>
> If she chooses the tee: “I'll show one. Your job is to send the ball into that open space. Want another try or a break?”
>
> After a miss: “Let's reset. Would you like another go?”

Keep your tone matter-of-fact; do not announce that she is scared, lazy, anxious, or “a visual learner.” Speak with the guardian privately about practical support if needed, without asking for a diagnosis or building a child profile. AAP guidance supports adapting basic sports tasks to readiness and focusing on learning and enjoyment; it does not assess this individual child. [AAP sports readiness guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/sports/Pages/Is-Your-Child-Ready-for-Sports.aspx), accessed October 11, 2026.

If you later choose to seek video feedback, first follow guardian permission, the child's willingness, league media policy, and the recipient platform's privacy controls. Prefer the minimum clip necessary with names, jersey identifiers, team/location details, other children, and unnecessary audio removed. Do not post it publicly. A clip may show a few visible actions; it cannot reliably establish pain, intent, eye fixation, calibrated angles, injury risk, or a diagnosis—especially from one attempt or a cropped/blurry view.
