Here is wording the assistants can use, followed by the reasoning and a practical next step.

**(a) Close hardball front toss without a protective screen**

“No—we will not run that setup. Being careful does not protect the feeder from a hard line drive. Use the tee today, or arrange a suitable soft-ball activity with safe separation and required protection. Hardball front toss needs an appropriate, properly positioned protective screen.”

This is a conservative coaching control, not a claim that every league has an identical front-toss rule. Check the governing organization's equipment and practice requirements. A screen addresses batted-ball exposure; it does not make it acceptable to feed within the bat's reach. Keep **every part of the feeder, including the feeding hand, outside the hitter's full swing reach**. Do not substitute “duck quickly,” a glove, or confidence for a protective arrangement. Close distance gives the feeder less time to react; an unexpected mishit can still travel toward the adult.

For today's tee alternative: one ready batter in the swing area, a fitted required helmet and permitted manageable bat, soft ball on a tee, and an empty hitting sector. The coach adjusts the tee only with the bat parked, then moves clear before authorizing a swing. Waiting children have empty hands and stay outside swing reach and possible ball paths. No child catcher. Park the bat and stop all activity before collection; any ball entering another activity's space means stopping that activity too. If those controls or required gear are unavailable, do a ball-free movement activity instead.

Little League's current Safety Code specifically requires protective NOCSAE batting helmets during practice and games and restricts on-deck bat handling in Tee Ball, Minor, and Major divisions. Those provisions support the equipment controls; they are not being cited as a universal screen mandate. [Little League Safety Code, checked October 11, 2026](https://www.littleleague.org/playing-rules/appendices/appendix-b/).

**(b) Crossed first base and turned left: automatically out?**

“No—turning left alone is not enough information to declare an automatic out. We need the league's applicable rule and what the runner actually did.”

Before deciding the play, establish the organization, baseball division, season and competition type, and local rules. Also establish whether this was a batter-runner who touched first safely, whether the ball was live, whether the runner returned immediately or attempted to advance toward second, and whether a legal tag or relevant appeal occurred. “Crossed first” does not by itself confirm the base was touched.

As a scoped, current example, the **2026 Official Baseball Rules**, Rules **5.09(b)(4)** and **5.09(b)(11)**, protect a batter-runner who overruns first and returns immediately. A runner attempting second can be put out by a tag; beginning that attempt does not itself record the out. A failure to return while heading toward the dugout or a fielding position has a separate appeal provision. My conclusion from that wording is that the relevant distinction is the runner's action, not a left-versus-right turn alone. These are professional baseball rules; a youth program may adopt or modify them. They do not resolve an unidentified league's play. [2026 Official Baseball Rules, printed pages 46 and 49; accessed October 11, 2026](https://mktg.mlbstatic.com/mlb/official-information/2026-official-baseball-rules.pdf).

For beginner instruction, say **“Touch first, run through into the clear space, slow down, and return promptly unless you're making a taught advance.”** Walk the route without balls, defenders, or tags first. Do not teach “left means out” or “always turn right and you're safe.” During a game, let the umpire apply the verified rule to the observed action; do not invent the missing facts from this short description.

**(c) A cropped, blurred still supposedly proves a harmful elbow angle**

“I haven't been shown an image, so I can't describe or measure this child's position. A cropped, blurred still also could not by itself prove that an elbow angle will cause injury. I would not prescribe an exact angle from it.”

An elbow angle depends on which anatomical points and plane are being measured and which moment of the delivery is shown. A two-dimensional camera view projects a three-dimensional movement; camera position, perspective, hidden landmarks, and blur can change the apparent angle. Cropping may remove the feet, trunk, ball, target, or release, and a single frame supplies no timing or sequence. Even a clear geometric measurement would describe that moment, not establish tissue load, symptoms, a diagnosis, or future injury for this child. An association reported in a study would not by itself prove individual causation either. I cannot infer pain, fatigue, eye fixation, or injury risk from an unseen image.

The practical adult response is to check comfort, workload, rest, equipment, footing, and understanding before changing mechanics. If the child reports pain or fatigue, stop throwing. Do not test a supposedly safer elbow angle or use a successful throw to clear return. A guardian should seek an appropriate healthcare professional for pain or injury concerns. AAP guidance explains that training load and recovery matter and that overuse injury may occur without obvious swelling; it does not supply a universal safe elbow angle. [AAP overuse guidance, updated January 22, 2024; checked October 11, 2026](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/sports-injuries/Pages/Preventing-Overuse-Injuries.aspx).

For a comfortable child whose activity is permitted, a qualified youth coach can observe ordinary, comparable attempts and use a manageable target with an easy cue such as **“Step toward the target.”** First change one task demand—such as a closer target—rather than forcing an elbow height or arm slot. Observe understandable outcomes like direction, controlled finish, and the child's report of comfort. This is instruction, not medical assessment or injury prediction. No public photo upload is needed; keep any family-approved review private and appropriate to the child's needs.

These answers do not validate the actual field, equipment installation, league ruling, or a child's medical status.
